# Feedback rubric

How to assess an answer in Step 5, and how to write the Step 6 debrief. The stance is **the interviewer's chair**: what would the person sitting across the table actually think and write down. Honest and specific. Not harsh, not a pep talk.

## Handling voice-transcribed answers

The user will often answer by voice, so the input arrives as a rough transcript. **Judge content, structure, and substance — not transcription mechanics.**

Ignore, always:

- filler words ("um," "like," "you know," "sort of")
- missing or wrong punctuation and capitalization
- homophones and mis-transcriptions ("their" / "there," a garbled product name)
- sentence fragments and false starts that are clearly artifacts of speaking
- a trailing-off sentence that the next sentence clearly completes

Still flag, because they're real:

- genuinely not answering the question that was asked
- no discernible structure — the interviewer can't follow the thread
- rambling: three minutes to say what needed thirty seconds
- burying the answer at the end instead of leading with it
- "we" throughout with no "I" — can't tell what *this person* did
- no specifics: no project named, no number, no decision, no tradeoff

If you genuinely can't tell whether something was a transcription glitch or a real stumble ("was that a real 'I don't know' or did the audio cut"), ask before scoring it.

## The dimensions

Weight varies by round (see `references/question-generation.md`), but every answer is read against these:

1. **Answered the actual question.** The most common failure by far. Did they address what was asked, or pivot to an adjacent thing they'd rather talk about? An interviewer notices immediately and it reads as either not listening or dodging.
2. **Structure.** Could the interviewer follow it without effort? Behavioral answers: situation → what *you* did → result, with the point up front. A buried lede ("...and so that's how we eventually tripled activation" as the last sentence) wastes the whole answer.
3. **Specificity and evidence.** A real project, a real number, a real tradeoff, a real decision *they* made. Vague competence ("I'm very data-driven and collaborative") carries zero signal — anyone can say it.
4. **Signal for this role.** Did the answer demonstrate one of the JD's real requirements, or just general professionalism? A strong answer to the wrong question still doesn't advance them.
5. **Self-awareness.** On failure / weakness / feedback questions: real ownership, a real lesson, no deflection ("my weakness is I care too much," "the failure was really the other team's fault").
6. **Concision.** A behavioral answer should land in about two minutes. Past that, the interviewer is waiting for it to end and has stopped absorbing.
7. **Engagement.** For "why us" and for their own questions: evidence they've actually thought about the company and the role, not interchangeable enthusiasm.

## The scorecard line (after each answer)

One or two sentences of what an interviewer would actually write. Concrete, and honest about the unflattering read when that's the true one. Examples:

- *"Strong story, real numbers, clear that she drove it. Would advance on this answer alone."*
- *"Answered a broader, easier question than the one I asked. Still don't know what he specifically decided — kept saying 'the team.'"*
- *"Three minutes, no structure, and the actual result came in the last sentence. The substance was there but I had to dig for it."*
- *"Textbook 'my weakness is perfectionism.' Didn't believe it, and now I'm going to push harder on the failure question."*

Then: **the single highest-value fix** — the one change that moves this answer most. One thing, not a list.

Then, when warranted: **the follow-up probe** a real interviewer would now ask — and let them answer it.

## Honest, not harsh

- Harsh: "That was a bad answer." Honest: "That answer doesn't tell me what *you* did — an interviewer leaves it not able to advocate for you."
- Say what landed when something lands, in a line — then move on. Don't manufacture a positive to cushion every note; the user came for the real read.
- Never soften the overall debrief read to be encouraging. If this round would be a "lean no," say "lean no" and say exactly why. That's the most useful thing in the whole session.
- The tone is a colleague who wants them to get the job telling them the truth before it costs them the offer — direct, specific, no padding, no cruelty.

## The debrief (Step 6)

The written deliverable. Structure it as:

- **The honest read** — *clear advance / lean advance / on the fence / lean no / clear no*, one-sentence reason.
- **What came through** — strengths that actually landed, each tied to the answer that showed it.
- **What cost the most signal** — ranked, concrete, tied to specific answers.
- **Patterns across answers** — the recurring tells a single-answer note misses (buries the answer, no numbers, "we" not "I", one story reused, runs long, flat "why us").
- **Prep actions** — few and specific. "Rewrite the [X] story so the decision and the number are in the first two sentences" beats "work on structure."
- **Questions to ask the interviewer** — three or four, tailored to this round and interviewer.
- **A one-line closing appeal** — see below.

If a `<name>-voice` skill exists, write the debrief through it — it should read like a straight note from a friend who watched the interview, not a form.

## The closing appeal

The last thing in the debrief, and its own kind of output — not another prep action. One sentence the person could actually say near the end of *this specific interview* (when asked "anything else you want to add?" or as the last line before goodbyes), or fold into a same-day follow-up note. It names the fit between them and the role in a single breath.

**Derive it from the mock, not from the resume.** Pull the fit point that showed the most signal in *this session* — usually whichever item under "What came through" landed hardest, or the moment an answer connected a real thing they've done to one of the JD's real requirements without you having to explain the connection. Don't synthesize a from-scratch elevator pitch off their whole career; that's what the "why us" answer skill is for, and it produces something too generic to feel earned by this specific conversation.

**One sentence, plain register, no hedging.** This is spoken, so run it through the same ear as a video-intro close (`job-application-video-intro-script`'s spoken-not-written checks apply): no "I believe I would be a strong fit for this position" — say the specific thing plainly. E.g., for an ops-and-AI-fluency screen: *"That gap you're describing between the pilot and the actual workflow — that's the exact gap I closed at Sendbird twice, so I'd want to keep talking."* Not: *"I'm confident my background makes me a great fit for this AI Strategist role."*

**Anchor it to something concrete from their answer** — a company, a workflow, a number, a tool they use daily — tied directly to the specific requirement it answers, not a mashup of the whole resume.

**Say why it's the right note to end the debrief on** in a short line before or after it — what it's doing for them (e.g., "this is the connection Brett didn't have to be told twice, worth landing explicitly since the round is short and he won't dig for it himself").
